Film-Induced Tourism: How Films & OTT Series Turn Viewers into Travellers

Film-induced tourism refers to travel that is initiated by a place that a person saw on screen, in a film, a TV programme, an OTT series, or in a music video. Such visitors go to see the locations where the films were shot, to reenact a scene or to stay in the places where the characters did. The terms movie tourism and set-jetting refer to the same behaviour; set-jetting is now the term used for it.

The scale has changed in the last few years. In Expedia's Unpack '26 set-jetting research, a OnePoll survey of 24,000 adults across 18 countries, 81% of travel agents from Gen Z and the millennial generation said that they plan their trips around film and television locations, and 53% of all travellers reported that their interest in holidays inspired by films and television had increased.

For a destination marketer, that puts set-jetting tourism in the same planning cycle as paid media, PR and content.

Key takeaways

  • 81% of Gen Z and millennial travellers plan trips around film and TV locations.
  • OTT's same-day global releases and binge viewing create faster, wider demand spikes than cinema.
  • Viewers move from "where was it filmed" to stays and packages, so the first page a destination needs is a location page.
  • India, GCC and global audiences book differently; plan content, platforms and offers per market.

Why OTT reaches travellers faster than cinema

A theatrical film still rolls out market by market. Release dates can differ by weeks between the US, India and the Gulf, so interest in a location builds slowly and unevenly.

Streaming removed that delay. Netflix, Prime Video and Disney+ usually drop an original in every market they serve on the same day, so a viewer in Mumbai, Dubai and London sees the same coastline in the same week. Search demand for that place rises everywhere at once.

The effect is easy to measure on hit shows. After the first teaser for season three of The White Lotus, Hotels.com recorded a 65% jump in searches for the Four Seasons Resort Koh Samui, as ABC News reported. Expedia said the first two seasons lifted travel demand to Hawaii and Sicily by 300% within 90 days of airing.

Binge-watching and repeat exposure

A two-hour film gives a location a few scenes. An eight-episode series can give it six to ten hours of screen time, often watched over one weekend. That repetition is what moves a place from "looks nice" to "I want to go". Series also return for new seasons and get rewatched, so the location keeps resurfacing long after launch week.

Screen to search: how viewers research filming locations

Most set-jetters follow the same query path, and each step is a chance for a destination to show up.

  1. Curiosity: "where was The White Lotus season 3 filmed" or "ZNMD Spain locations".
  2. Place: the town, beach, fort or hotel named in the answer, plus "how to get there" and "best time to visit".
  3. Stay and do: hotels near the location, tours, itineraries and packages.
  4. Proof: reels, vlogs and reviews that show what the place looks like off-camera.

The channels split by stage. Google deals with questions relating to 'where' and 'how', while YouTube and Instagram respond to 'what it's like' by using walkthroughs and reels. Now, people use AI for travel planning; for instance, AI assistants such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Google's AI Overviews provide a direct answer to the first question, in which case the destination mentioned in that answer gets the click.

What we see at Melange: on travel accounts, the first query after a release is almost always about the location, not the booking. The brands that capture that traffic are the ones with a page that answers "where was it filmed" and then links to stays and experiences. Brands that only publish package pages tend to miss the first wave entirely.

Reels, fan edits and scene recreations: the second wave

A release window lasts a few weeks. The short-form content around it can run for years.

Fans cut edits of favourite scenes, creators film themselves recreating a shot at the real spot, and travel pages post "I went to the Dil Chahta Hai fort" reels. Each clip puts the location back in front of people who may never have watched the original. Chapora Fort in North Goa still draws visitors who come for that one frame from a 2001 film.

For marketers, this second wave is easier to shape than the release itself. Destinations can seed creator trips, publish the exact viewpoint and best light, and repost fan content with permission.

Movie tourism in India, the GCC and global markets

The screen-to-trip pattern holds true everywhere, but the factors that set it off and the ways in which people book vary from market to market. This is the way in which we understand the three audiences that we most frequently plan for.

  • India: triggered by Bollywood films, Indian OTT originals and song sequences. Travellers go in friend or family groups, plan around long weekends and festival holidays, watch prices closely and book heavily through OTAs. Research happens on YouTube, Instagram, Google and OTA apps.
  • GCC: sparked by Hollywood or regional productions shot locally, Arabic series and celebrity tourism campaigns. Travellers lean towards premium stays, short-haul luxury breaks and family trips with higher spend. Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat and Google carry most of the research.
  • Global (US, UK, EU): driven by prestige OTT series and franchise films. Gen Z and millennials lead, often solo or as couples, booking tours and stays tied to a specific show. TikTok, Instagram, Google, and AI assistants are where planning starts.

Film tourism in India: ZNMD and Spain

Zindagi Na Milegi Dobara (2011) took three friends on a road trip through Catalonia, Andalusia and Pamplona. The year after release, 60,444 Indians visited Spain, close to double the 2011 figure, according to Turespaña figures reported by The Local. Indian demand still runs through OTAs, which is why pairing content with OTA and search campaigns matters; our performance marketing work with MakeMyTrip shows how that side works.

Singapore Tourism Board: Jigra and the Indian audience

Jigra (2024) shot key sequences in Singapore at the Helix Bridge, Singapore River, Raffles Place and National Gallery Singapore, according to Bollywood Hungama. It was Alia Bhatt's third Singapore-shot film. That repeat exposure makes the city easy to package as a "Jigra route" itinerary for Indian families and first-time visitors.

GCC: AlUla as a film destination

Saudi Arabia built film tourism into AlUla's plan from the start. Film AlUla hosted 85 productions in 2024 and 83 in 2025, per the Saudi Press Agency, including the Gerard Butler thriller Kandahar. Visit Saudi now publishes a guide to the country's movie locations, which is the kind of owned asset that turns screen interest into itineraries.

Film tourism marketing: turning screen interest into bookings

Film-induced tourism only pays off when there is somewhere for the interest to land. For DMOs, and NTOs, that means building the assets before or during a release, not months after.

  1. Location pages. One page per title answering "where was it filmed", with maps, access notes, opening hours and the best time to visit. This is the page AI engines and featured snippets quote.
  2. Itineraries. A two- or three-day route linking the filming spots with nearby food, stays and attractions, so a single scene becomes a full trip.
  3. "Stay where it was filmed" packages. Partner with the hotel, villa or resort on screen, or the closest match, and bundle it with guided tours.
  4. Paid and organic together. Use search campaigns on title and location terms while the organic pages build rankings, then shift budget as organic takes over.
  5. Branded content and IPs. Some destinations now co-create the content itself, from web series to creator-led formats. Melange's film tourism marketing services cover this end of destination marketing.

The downside: over-tourism and short-lived spikes

Screen fame can overwhelm a place. Dubrovnik's overnight stays roughly doubled to about 4 million between 2012 and 2019 as Game of Thrones aired, according to Architizer, and the city later limited daily cruise ship arrivals. Other locations see a sharp spike that fades within a season. Plan for both: spread demand across several sites, set capacity limits at fragile spots, and brief local communities early.

Conclusion

Film-induced tourism has moved from occasional windfall to a repeatable pattern. OTT releases reach every market at once, reels keep locations in view for years, and AI assistants now answer the first "where was it filmed" question. Destinations that prepare location pages, itineraries and partner packages, and manage the crowds that follow, turn viewers into travellers. FAQs

What is an example of film-induced tourism in India?

Chapora Fort in Goa became a fixture on tourist routes after Dil Chahta Hai (2001). Other examples include Ladakh's Pangong Lake after 3 Idiots and Jaisalmer's Sonar Kella after Satyajit Ray's film of the same name.

How do OTT platforms influence travel decisions?

They release shows in every market on the same day and encourage binge viewing, so audiences spend hours with one location in a short window. That repeated exposure turns interest into searches for flights, hotels, and itineraries within weeks of release.

What is set-jetting?

Set-jetting is travelling to a destination because it appeared in a film or TV series. The term is used interchangeably with film-induced tourism and set-jetting tourism.

Which destinations are trending for set-jetting in 2026?

Expedia's 2026 list includes Yorkshire, Tuscany, Samoa, Croatia's Dalmatian Coast, the Peloponnese in Greece, Los Angeles, Wellington and Palawan in the Philippines, each linked to a new film or series.

How can a DMO measure the impact of film tourism?

Track branded and location search volume in Google Search Console and Google Trends before and after release, visits to location pages, OTA and hotel booking data for the area, and visitor counts at the filming sites.
