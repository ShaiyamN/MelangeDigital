Destination Marketing Strategies: How to Choose the Right One for Your Goal

The best destination marketing strategies begin with a goal rather than a channel. A destination that is virtually unknown to travellers will require a very different approach than one that people are aware of but have a mistaken perception of. Both situations require a different approach from a destination launching a new air route. The right strategy depends on what needs to change, which source market the destination is targeting, and how familiar travellers in that market already are with it.

With 1.52 billion international tourist arrivals recorded by UN Tourism in 2025, every destination is competing for attention. This guide sets out six common goals, the strategy that fits each, and how to measure the result.

Key takeaways

  • Choose the strategy following on from the goal and the source market having been defined, not before.
  • Unfamiliar destinations should win over the travel trade prior to any spending on consumer campaigns.
  • Perception problems need demonstrable proof, not more advertising.
  • One main KPI per goal with a baseline before launch.

Start with the goal, not the channel

Many tourism boards will go into their planning for next year by just taking a destination that had a good year and copying it. That rarely works. A campaign that worked in a well-known city in a developed market is extremely unlikely to work in a destination that the traveller just can't place.

Researchers Eli Avraham and Eran Ketter made a similar case in a 2015 paper in Tourism: An International Interdisciplinary Journal. They explain that destinations pursue different marketing goals, from building awareness in new markets to recovering from a crisis, and each goal calls for a different approach. 

A good DMO marketing strategy starts with one question really: what needs to change in the market before it will book us? The channels, the creators and the budget all come from the answer to that.

Destination marketing strategies for six common goals

Building awareness for an unfamiliar destination

When travellers are unfamiliar with a destination, consumer advertising alone may not be enough to generate interest. You have to start with the people who sell travel i.e. the tour operators, the travel agents, the DMCs and the MICE planners. They need the product knowledge, the rates and the contacts before they're going to recommend anything. The trade shows, the sales missions, the agent training come first. The creators come after that and that's where you give the destination a face that the market can relate to.

Zambia Tourism Authority took this route into India. Its first appearance at OTM Mumbai 2026, delivered by Melange, won the Most Promising Destination award, and the authority reported more than 500 trade connections over its one-year mandate.

Changing an outdated perception

Some destinations are well known for the wrong thing. The task here is a destination branding strategy that replaces one image with another, backed by proof travellers can see for themselves. Saying the same thing louder rarely shifts a settled view.

Saudi Arabia faced this in India, where many travellers saw it mainly as a religious destination. Melange's mall activation for Saudi tourism at Phoenix Marketcity put the country's leisure side in front of families for four days, through a Saudi-style kiosk, a destination photo booth and a lead-capture game, with interested visitors handed straight to a travel partner. Experiential marketing suits perception work because people remember what they stood inside.

Launching a new route, product or season

The trade and media need to see a new direct flight, cruise deployment or shoulder season before consumers believe it. Co-marketing with the airline or cruise line splits the cost and taps into their existing customer base. Hosted FAM trips and travel PR give agents and journalists first-hand stories to sell and publish. Time the trip so guests leave with a sales kit before the main booking window opens.

Reaching a new audience segment

Destinations can find new growth opportunities by reaching traveller groups they have not fully engaged with, such as under-35s, women travelling together, or affluent families in smaller cities. Each audience has its own preferences, trusted voices and preferred platforms.

Singapore Tourism Board's #IsThisEvenSingapore campaign cast creators from five subcultures, including hip-hop and cricket, to reach young Indian travellers. The under-35 share of Indian arrivals rose from 31% to 39%. For Resorts World Sentosa, a regional creator campaign on ShareChat and Moj across 15 Indian states produced 4,927 direct bookings through a tracked link. Our guide to travel influencer marketing explains how to vet creators for this kind of work.

Staying top of mind in a mature market

In a mature market, even a well-known destination can start to feel familiar. Film, music and OTT integrations offer a way to keep it relevant by featuring it in the stories audiences already choose to watch.

For Singapore Tourism Board's partnership with Dharma Productions on Jigra, more than half of the film was shot in Singapore across seven landmarks, and it led to over six follow-on production conversations. Treat branded content and IP as a multi-year asset rather than a one-off campaign.

Recovering after a crisis

Natural disasters, health scares and political events can stop demand overnight. Recovery marketing has to answer safety and access questions first, then give travellers a reason to come back now. After COVID-19, Iceland's government funded a run of Inspired by Iceland campaigns from 2020 to 2022 to support the tourism industry's recovery. Relaunch only when the destination can deliver what the campaign promises, and brief trade partners so they can answer travellers' questions.

How to choose your strategy in four steps

  1. Name the source market and the traveller segment you want.
  2. Find out what that segment knows and believes about you, through trade conversations, search data and brand tracking.
  3. Pick the goal that closes the biggest gap.
  4. Match the strategy, then set the KPI and baseline.

Turning the goal into a tourism marketing strategy you can measure

Agree one main KPI for each goal before launch, so the board reports change rather than activity:


  • Awareness: trade connections, agent sales and branded search demand from the market
  • Perception change: brand-tracking scores, sentiment and enquiries
  • New route or product: bookings on trip-specific codes and seats or cabins sold
  • New segment: that segment's share of arrivals
  • Mature market: length of stay, spend and repeat visits
  • Crisis recovery: arrivals against pre-crisis levels

Views and engagement can help assess how well a campaign's creative performs, but they should not be the only measures of success. Tourism boards should also track outcomes that relate directly to their objectives, such as bookings, visitor arrivals or changes in brand perception. Choosing destination marketing strategies that fit

The right destination marketing strategies come from a clear goal in a clearly defined market, with a measure agreed in advance. Get the diagnosis right and the choice of trade, creators, activations or film becomes much easier to defend. See how Melange has applied this for tourism boards in our work.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main destination marketing strategies?

Common destination marketing strategies include travel trade marketing, trade shows, influencer partnerships, FAM trips, PR, experiential campaigns, film and branded content, airline or OTA partnerships, and search marketing. The right mix depends on the destination's objectives, target audience and source market.

How do you choose a destination marketing strategy?

Identify the source market and target segment and determine what knowledge and beliefs they have regarding the destination. Decide on the largest gap to bridge (awareness, changing perceptions etc.) and determine which strategies and KPIs will help achieve this.

How is a DMO marketing strategy different from a brand campaign?

A DMO marketing strategy is a broader plan for achieving destination marketing goals across different markets, traveller segments and partners. A brand campaign is usually a more focused initiative within that strategy, with specific messaging, activities and success measures.

What is a destination branding strategy?

A destination branding strategy defines how a place wants to be seen by its target travellers and the proof points that support that image. It guides every campaign, so messages stay consistent across markets and partners.

How should tourism boards measure destination marketing?

Set one main KPI per goal before launch and record a baseline. Useful measures include arrivals by segment, agent sales, bookings on tracked codes, branded search demand, length of stay and brand-tracking scores, with reach and engagement used as supporting indicators.
